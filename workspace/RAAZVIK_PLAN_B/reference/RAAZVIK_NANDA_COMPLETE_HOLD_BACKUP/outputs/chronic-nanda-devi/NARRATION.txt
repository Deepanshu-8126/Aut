In 1965, a team of Indian and American climbers was forced down a mountain in the Himalayas. Tied to the mountain behind them was a secret device powered by plutonium.

They planned to return for it. When they did, it was gone.

This was not a lost nuclear bomb. It was the power source for a spying station, carried up Nanda Devi to help monitor China. More than sixty years later, the question is not just where it went. It's what happened to the radioactive material inside.

In October 1964, China tested its first atomic bomb. Understanding what China might do next became a pressing intelligence problem for the United States. And for India, which had fought a border war with China two years earlier, the same question was uncomfortably close to home.

One proposal was to take listening equipment high into the Himalayas. There, an unattended station might pick up signals associated with Chinese missile tests. The people carrying it would not be ordinary spies. They would be mountaineers.

The mountain was Nanda Devi, in what is now the Indian state of Uttarakhand. Nearly twenty-six thousand feet high, it was a formidable choice. The joint effort assembled Indian and American climbers, Sherpas, porters and technical specialists for an expedition whose real purpose was kept secret.

The Indian team was led by Manmohan Singh Kohli, who had recently led India's successful 1965 Everest expedition. Now his climbers had a different task: put an electronic listening station on a mountain, and leave it running.

The station included an antenna and monitoring equipment. Its power source was a radioisotope thermoelectric generator, or RTG. Reports identify the model as SNAP nineteen C.

An RTG is not a nuclear bomb. It isn't a reactor sustaining a chain reaction, either. Radioactive material releases heat as it decays. The generator converts some of that heat into electricity, allowing equipment to work where ordinary refuelling would be difficult.

In this case, the fuel was plutonium, contained in sealed capsules. Published accounts differ on the exact quantity and isotopic makeup. What matters here is that a source of radioactive heat was being carried toward the high camp.

Climbers later recalled that the power source gave off warmth. On a freezing mountain, the equipment's heat could be felt. But the material producing it was also why its loss would matter long after the expedition ended.

In autumn 1965, the team made its way up Nanda Devi through successive camps. Then the weather turned. A storm made the final push too dangerous.

Near Camp Four, the climbers faced a choice: keep struggling upward, try to bring every piece of equipment down immediately, or secure it and get off the mountain. Kohli ordered a retreat. The apparatus was anchored near the high camp, with the intention of returning after winter.

It was a decision made to protect the people on the mountain, not a plan to discard the equipment permanently. For months, snow, ice and rock moved through a place the team could not watch. The station never completed its planned mission on Nanda Devi.

In the spring of 1966, a team climbed back toward the cache. They expected to find the equipment where it had been secured. Instead, according to later accounts, the site had drastically changed. The generator was missing.

The climbers suspected that an avalanche or a collapse of snow, ice and rock had carried it away. That is a plausible explanation, not a witnessed moment. No one was there watching the exact instant it disappeared.

The search continued. Indian and American teams returned in later seasons, scanning dangerous ground with detection equipment. The original generator was not publicly recovered, and no search established its exact location.

Think about what that means. The climbers knew where they had left it. They did not know where the mountain had taken it. On a shifting glacier, beneath snow and debris, even a substantial metal object could become extraordinarily hard to find.

The wider intelligence project continued. In 1967, another station was placed on nearby Nanda Kot. It operated for a period and then ran into its own problems.

This is where the story can get confused: Nanda Kot was a different mountain, and the station there was a different device. The later recovery of equipment from that operation does not mean the missing Nanda Devi generator was found.

By the late 1970s, reporting had exposed the operation. India's prime minister, Morarji Desai, acknowledged it in Parliament. A secret shared between two governments had become a public question: what had been left behind, and who was responsible for finding it?

The concern reached beyond the mountain. Water from this region feeds rivers connected to the Ganges system. But concern is not the same as proof of a leak. The crucial unknowns are the generator's location, the condition of its capsules, and whether any material escaped.

Plutonium is hazardous if particles are inhaled or ingested. The risk from a release would depend on where, how much and in what form it spread. It would be irresponsible to say a river has been poisoned without reliable evidence. Reports describe earlier water testing that found no contamination; that is not a complete picture of every location today.

There is no established evidence that the lost generator caused a Himalayan flood. And the absence of proof of a leak is not a photograph of the missing capsules, either.

The mission began with a plan to listen for secrets across a border. Instead, it left a secret of its own on a mountain.

We know why the station was carried upward. We know why the climbers came down. And when they returned, the equipment was missing. What happened in between is an inference. Its present location and condition are not publicly established.

Under ice, beneath rock, or somewhere no search has reached, the answer remains open. Not a prediction of catastrophe. Just the question the mountain has never answered.

Where did it go?
